AMINE BASICITY ORDERS & DIAGNOSTIC DISTINCTION TESTS

Gas vs Aqueous Phase Hierarchy, Aniline Resonance Suppression, Hinsberg Separation, and Carbylamine Fingerprint

1. Amine Basicity: Gas vs Aqueous Phase
Inductive vs Hydration
Gas Phase Order (Pure Inductive +I Effect)
In gas phase without solvent hydration, basicity depends solely on electron-density release by alkyl groups onto nitrogen:
GAS PHASE: 3° Amine > 2° Amine > 1° Amine > NH₃
Aqueous Phase Interplay (Three Competing Factors)
Dictated by combined balance of: (1) +I Inductive effect, (2) Steric hindrance of alkyl groups, and (3) Hydration energy of substituted ammonium cation (1° cation has 3 H-bonds with water, 2° has 2, 3° has only 1).
METHYL SERIES (213 Rule): (CH₃)₂NH (2°) > CH₃NH₂ (1°) > (CH₃)₃N (3°) > NH₃
ETHYL SERIES (231 Rule): (C₂H₅)₂NH (2°) > (C₂H₅)₃N (3°) > C₂H₅NH₂ (1°) > NH₃
Key Rule: Secondary amines (2°) are ALWAYS the strongest bases in aqueous solution!
2. Aniline Basicity Suppression & Substituent Effects
Resonance vs Protonation
Why Aniline is a Very Weak Base (pKb = 9.38)
• Resonance Delocalization: The nitrogen lone pair delocalizes into the benzene ring (+R effect) across 5 canonical structures, drastically diminishing electron density on nitrogen.
• Anilinium Destabilization: Aniline has 5 resonance structures, whereas protonated anilinium ion (C₆H₅NH₃⁺) has only 2 structures! Protonation reduces resonance stability.
Ring Substituents on Basicity (CUET Ranking)
• Electron-Withdrawing Groups (-I, -M): (-NO₂, -CN, -X) severely decrease basicity. Nitro group at ortho/para position withdraws lone pair most powerfully.
• Electron-Donating Groups (+I, +M): (-OCH₃, -CH₃) pump electron density, increasing basicity (p-methoxyaniline > aniline).
• Ortho Effect: Ortho-substituted anilines are almost always weaker bases than aniline, regardless of whether group is EWG or EDG!
3. The Hinsberg Test (Distinction of 1°, 2°, 3°)
Benzenesulphonyl Chloride
Reagent: Benzenesulphonyl Chloride (C₆H₅SO₂Cl)
Used universally to detect, distinguish, and separate mixtures of primary, secondary, and tertiary amines based on acidic N-H protons.
1° Amine → Soluble in Aqueous KOH
Forms N-alkylbenzenesulphonamide. Because of the strong electron-withdrawing SO₂ group, the remaining hydrogen attached to nitrogen is strongly acidic. It dissolves in aqueous KOH to form a soluble salt. Acidification reprecipitates it.
2° Amine → Insoluble in Aqueous KOH
Forms N,N-dialkylbenzenesulphonamide. It contains no acidic hydrogen attached to nitrogen; hence it is insoluble in aqueous KOH and remains an insoluble solid.
3° Amine → No Reaction
Tertiary amines possess no hydrogen atom on nitrogen and do not react with benzenesulphonyl chloride. Remains insoluble in alkali, dissolves in dilute HCl.
4. Carbylamine & Nitrous Acid Fingerprints
Isocyanides & Diazonium
Carbylamine Reaction (Isocyanide Test for 1° Amines)
Warming any primary amine (aliphatic or aromatic) with chloroform (CHCl₃) and ethanolic KOH produces an alkyl/aryl isocyanide (carbylamine, R-NC):
R-NH₂ + CHCl₃ + 3 KOH → R-NC (Intolerable Foul Odour) + 3 KCl + 3 H₂O
CUET Distinction: Secondary and tertiary amines do NOT give this test!
Reaction with Nitrous Acid (HNO₂ / NaNO₂ + HCl)
• 1° Aliphatic Amine: Yields unstable alkyldiazonium salt that decomposes instantly with brisk effervescence of N₂ gas and forms alcohols.
• 1° Aromatic Amine (Aniline): At 273–278 K forms stable Benzenediazonium Chloride (C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻).
• 2° Amines: Form yellow oily N-nitrosamines (R₂N-NO) (Liebermann's nitroso test).
• 3° Aliphatic: Form soluble nitrite salts; aromatic yields p-nitroso derivative.
CUET VERDICT Aqueous basicity: 2° is always #1 (213 for methyl, 231 for ethyl). Hinsberg: 1° dissolves in KOH, 2° is insoluble, 3° no reaction. Carbylamine is exclusive to 1° amines!
