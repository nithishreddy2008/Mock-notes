The Decipherment of Early Scripts (Prinsep's Breakthrough)
Methodological Milestones
Step 01 Late 18th – 1830s
Bilingual Coin Analysis
European scholars examined coins of Indo-Greek rulers containing Greek on the obverse and Kharosthi on the reverse. Comparing known Greek royal names (e.g., Apollodotus) allowed letter-by-letter phonetic matching.
Step 02 1838 Milestone
Prinsep Deciphers Asokan Brahmi
James Prinsep (mint officer, Asiatic Society of Bengal) deciphered Brahmi by matching recurring votive phrases on Sanchi stupa railings: matching words like danam ("gift") with Sanskrit/Prakrit equivalents.
Step 03 Epigraphic Cross-Check
The "Piyadassi" Mystery Solved
Most edicts referred to a king as Devanampiya Piyadassi ("Beloved of the Gods, of pleasant appearance"). Sri Lankan Buddhist chronicles (Dipavamsa & Mahavamsa) confirmed this title referred directly to Emperor Ashoka.
Step 04 Corpus Identification
Discovery of Named Edicts
Later discoveries at Maski (Karnataka), Gujarra (MP), Nittur, and Udegolam explicitly stated Devanampiya Asoka, confirming beyond all doubt the singular authorship of the pan-Indian edicts.
The Five Inherent Limitations of Epigraphic Evidence
Critical Historiography
Limit 01
Technical Damage & Weathering
Letters are often very faintly engraved; wind, rain, and stone fracturing have chipped away characters, forcing scholars to rely on uncertain reconstructions.
Limit 02
Uncertain Word Meanings
Ancient Prakrit and regional dialects contain idioms and administrative technical terms whose precise situational meaning is no longer known today.
Limit 03
Selective Survival Rate
Thousands of inscriptions were commissioned on perishable palm leaf, cloth, or destroyed stone; only a tiny fraction survived modern discovery and documentation.
Limit 04
Elite & Royal Commission Bias
Inscriptions recorded only what the wealthy commissioners (kings, queens, rich guilds) wanted displayed; royal defeats, famines, and tax burdens are rarely recorded.
Limit 05
Silence on Subaltern Lives
Ordinary agricultural labourers, marginal peasants, forest dwellers, and artisans left no stone records; daily hardships remain completely unrecorded in formal epigraphy.
Epigrapher's Rule: An inscription does not automatically provide impartial historical truth; it mirrors the political ambitions and worldview of its patron.
