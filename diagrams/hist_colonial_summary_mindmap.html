1. Permanent Settlement & Zamindars
1793 · BENGAL
  • Enactment: Introduced by Lord Charles Cornwallis (1793); fixed state revenue demand in perpetuity.
  • Why Defaults Occurred: Initial demands were artificially high; agricultural prices were low; Sunset Law enforced auction of defaulted estates.
  • Burdwan Auction (1797): Famous auction of Raja of Burdwan's estates, exposing widespread fictitious sales.
  • Benami Tactics: Zamindars transferred lands to women (exempt from seizure) and used agents to repeatedly buy back estates at auction.
★ Core Axis: Revenue Intermediaries & Jotedars
RURAL SOCIAL DYNAMICS
  • Rise of Jotedars: Rich resident peasants in North Bengal (Dinajpur, Rangpur); also called haoladars, gantidars, or mandals.
  • Sharecroppers (Adhiyars): Cultivated land via sharecroppers (bargadars) who handed over half the produce.
  • Direct Village Control: Unlike absentee urban zamindars, jotedars lived in villages and directly mobilized peasants to delay revenue payments.
  • Buying Out Zamindars: Jotedars eagerly bought up auctioned zamindari lands, steadily undermining traditional landed aristocracy.
2. The Fifth Report & Archives
1813 CE
  • Parliamentary Submission: Fifth of a series of reports submitted to the British Parliament in 1813 on Company administration.
  • Documentary Substance: Contained 1,002 pages, with over 800 pages reproducing petitions of zamindars, collectors' reports, and revenue accounts.
  • Political Agenda: Crafted by a Select Committee to criticize the East India Company's monopoly, exaggerating the collapse of zamindaris.
  • Official Bias: Reflected official colonial administrative views and neglected subaltern peasant perspectives.
3. The Hoe & Plough: Rajmahal Hills
ECOLOGICAL CONFLICT
  • Francis Buchanan's Surveys: Company surgeon and surveyor; observed landscapes through the lens of colonial commercial utility.
  • Paharias (The Hoe): Hill folk living around Rajmahal; shifting cultivators, gatherers of mahua/silk, resistant to colonial pacification.
  • Santhals (The Plough): Settled cultivators invited by British to clear lowland forests and establish settled agriculture.
  • Damin-i-Koh (1832): Enclosed territory demarcated with masonry boundary pillars for exclusive Santhal settlement.
4. The Santhal Hul (Rebellion)
1855–1856 CE
  • Roots of Revolt: Moneylenders (dikus) charged 50% to 500% interest; state levied heavy cash taxes; zamindars encroached on Damin-i-Koh.
  • Leadership: Brothers Sidhu and Kanhu Murmu mobilized thousands of Santhals, declaring divine mandate from Thakurji.
  • Military Pacification: British dispatched armed troops; declared martial law; burnt Santhal villages to suppress the rebellion.
  • Administrative Concession: Creation of Santhal Pargana district (5,500 sq miles) with special tribal land laws.
5. Bombay Deccan & Deccan Riots
1875 UPRISING
  • Ryotwari Framework: Revenue settled directly with the ryot; based on Ricardian rent theory; revised every 30 years.
  • Cotton Boom (1861–65): American Civil War boosted cotton exports; moneylenders (sahukars / vanis) offered easy credit.
  • Post-War Slump: Cotton prices collapsed in 1865; Limitation Law of 1859 prompted moneylenders to compound debts and refuse new loans.
  • Deccan Riots: Began in Supa (Poona district) in May 1875; peasants seized and burnt debt bonds and account books (bahikhatas).
