Alexander von Humboldt (1805)
Species-Area Law

German naturalist Alexander von Humboldt observed that within a region, species richness increases with increasing explored area, but only up to a limit.

S = C · AZ   |   log S = log C + Z log A
S = Species Richness  ·  A = Area  ·  C = Y-intercept  ·  Z = Slope (Regression Coeff)
Shape of Curve: Rectangular hyperbola on linear scale; perfectly straight line on logarithmic scale.
Ecological Scale / Region Z-Value Range Significance
Small regions (plants in Britain, birds in California, molluscs in NY) 0.1 to 0.2 Standard baseline slope
Entire continents (all taxa combined, continental scale) 0.6 to 1.2 Steeper slope with large area
Frugivorous birds & mammals in tropical forests (all continents) 1.15 Extremely steep (High CUET Trap!)
Key Takeaway: The larger and more diverse the area sampled, the steeper the slope of regression (higher Z).
Latitudinal Gradients & Amazon
Global Patterns
Equator (0°) · Maximum Diversity → Temperate (40°–60°) → Poles (90°) · Minimum Diversity

NCERT Case: Colombia near equator has 1,400 bird species; New York (41°N) has 105; Greenland (71°N) has only 56; India (tropical latitudes) harbors >1,200 species.

Amazonian Rainforest (Lungs of the Planet) Greatest Biodiversity on Earth
• Plants: >40,000 species • Fishes: >3,000 species • Birds: 1,300 species • Mammals: 427 species • Amphibians: 427 species • Reptiles: 378 species • Invertebrates: >125,000 species (>2 million undescribed)
1. Speciation Time: Tropics remained undisturbed by glaciations for millions of years, enabling long uninterrupted evolutionary diversification.
2. Predictable Environment: Tropical climates are less seasonal, more constant and predictable, promoting niche specialization and higher richness.
3. Solar Energy: Tropics receive higher radiant solar energy year-round, generating higher productivity that supports diverse trophic food webs.
David Tilman (Minnesota plots): Plots with more species showed less year-to-year variation in total biomass.
Paul Ehrlich: Rivet Popper
Ecosystem Stability
Stanford Ecologist Paul Ehrlich's Airplane Analogy:

"Imagine an airplane (ecosystem) with all parts joined together using thousands of rivets (species). If passengers begin popping rivets to take home, flight safety is jeopardized."

Airplane ⇄ Global Ecosystem
Rivets ⇄ Individual Species
Popping a rivet ⇄ Extinction of a species
Rivet on seat / window ⇄ Loss of minor species
Rivet on airplane wing ⇄ Loss of KEYSTONE species
Critical CUET Distinction: Popping a few rivets from seats causes minor discomfort, but popping a rivet from the wings (loss of key ecosystem drivers/keystone species like pollinators or apex predators) causes immediate, catastrophic flight failure.
Robert May's Global Estimate: ~7 million total species globally; only 22% documented till date!
