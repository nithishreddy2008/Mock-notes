1
Traditional Security (State-Centric)
Military Threats, Sovereignty & Interstate Balance
External Security Dimensions Military Defense
  • Referent Object: The sovereign state, territorial integrity, and independence.
  • Primary Threat: External military aggression from rival states.
  • Core Mechanisms: Deterrence (preventing war), Defense (limiting damage), Balance of Power (military/economic parity), and Alliances (NATO, Warsaw Pact).
Internal Security Dimensions Domestic Order
  • Maintenance of domestic peace, law, and order within national borders.
  • Mitigating separatist insurrections, communal strife, and armed civil conflicts.
  • Historical focus: Western states prioritized external threats; newly independent post-colonial states faced acute internal separatist pressures.
Traditional Security & Cooperation Treaty Regimes
  • Disarmament: Biological Weapons Convention (BWC, 1972), Chemical Weapons Convention (CWC, 1992).
  • Arms Control: NPT (1968), Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty (ABM, 1972), SALT I/II, START I/II.
  • Confidence-Building Measures (CBMs): Military transparency, hotline communication, military observers.
2
Non-Traditional Security (Human-Centric)
Human Security, Global Public Bads & Transnational Crises
Human Security Foundations People-Centric
  • Referent Object: Individual human beings and communities (not state borders).
  • Narrow Concept: "Freedom from Fear" (protection from violent conflicts, state repression, crimes).
  • Broad Concept: "Freedom from Want" (protection from hunger, disease, natural disasters, economic deprivation).
New Sources of Global Threats Transnational
  • Terrorism: Asymmetric violence targeting unarmed civilians (e.g., 9/11 US attacks, 26/11 Mumbai attacks).
  • Human Rights Atrocities: Genocides, ethnic cleansing (e.g., Rwanda 1994, Srebrenica).
  • Global Poverty: Massive North-South inequality, agrarian distress, urban squalor.
  • Migration & Displacement: Refugees (crossing international borders) vs Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs).
  • Health Epidemics: HIV/AIDS, Ebola, Bird Flu, SARS, COVID-19 pandemics.
Cooperative Security Framework Multilateralism
  • Non-military transnational threats cannot be resolved through national armed forces.
  • Requires bilateral, regional, continental, and global cooperation (UN, WHO, civil society NGOs).
  • Force is sanctioned only as a collective, multilateral measure of last resort.
3
India's 4-Pillar Security Strategy
Integrated National Defense & Socio-Economic Architecture
Pillar 1: Military Capabilities Deterrence
  • Surrounded by nuclear-armed neighbors (China, Pakistan); fought wars in 1947–48, 1962, 1965, 1971, 1999.
  • Conducted peaceful nuclear explosion (Pokhran-I, 1974) and weapon tests (Pokhran-II, 1998).
  • Maintains Credible Minimum Deterrence & "No First Use" (NFU) nuclear posture.
Pillar 2: Strengthening Global Norms Multilateral
  • Championed Non-Aligned Movement (NAM) to preserve strategic autonomy during the Cold War.
  • Consistent advocacy for non-discriminatory universal nuclear disarmament.
  • Committed to United Nations peacekeeping, international climate conventions, and multilateral trade rules.
Pillar 3: Preserving Internal Cohesion Integration
  • Managing internal secessionist and insurgent pressures: Nagaland, Mizoram, Punjab, Jammu & Kashmir.
  • Democratic power-sharing, federal autonomy, constitutional protections for linguistic/ethnic minorities.
Pillar 4: Economic Transformation Development
  • Eradicating mass poverty, inequality, hunger, and disease to ensure genuine human security.
  • Balancing high defense expenditure with essential social sector allocations (health, education, rural development).
