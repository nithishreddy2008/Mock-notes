RAOULT'S LAW ON A GRAPH — IDEAL vs POSITIVE vs NEGATIVE DEVIATION
Dashed line = what Raoult's law predicts  ·  Solid curve = what the real solution does

IDEAL — curve sits ON the line

A–B pull = A–A pull = B–B pull — no one notices the mixing
ΔHmix = 0 (no heat change) and ΔVmix = 0 (no volume change)
Classic pair: benzene + toluene; also n-hexane + n-heptane, bromoethane + chloroethane
Raoult's law holds at every mole fraction, both components

POSITIVE — curve bulges ABOVE

A–B pull is WEAKER than A–A and B–B — molecules escape more easily
Vapour pressure higher than predicted; ΔHmix > 0, ΔVmix > 0
Classic pairs: ethanol + acetone, CS₂ + acetone, water + ethanol
Ends the plot with a minimum-boiling azeotrope (ethanol–water 95%)

NEGATIVE — curve dips BELOW

A–B pull is STRONGER — molecules hold each other back (often H-bonding)
Vapour pressure lower than predicted; ΔHmix < 0, ΔVmix < 0
Classic pairs: chloroform + acetone, phenol + aniline, HCl + water, nitric acid + water
Ends the plot with a maximum-boiling azeotrope (68% HNO₃ + water)

WHY CHLOROFORM + ACETONE SHOWS A NEGATIVE DEVIATION

New H-bond forms: the chloroform H and the acetone O lock together — an attraction neither pure liquid had
Result: molecules stick harder, escape less, vapour pressure drops, and the mixture boils ABOVE both pure liquids

ETHANOL + ACETONE SHOWS A POSITIVE DEVIATION

Old H-bonds break: acetone molecules get between ethanol molecules and weaken the ethanol–ethanol hydrogen bonds
Result: molecules escape more freely, vapour pressure rises, and the mixture boils BELOW both pure liquids (95% ethanol azeotrope)
