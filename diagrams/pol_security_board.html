CONTEMPORARY SECURITY PARADIGMS — THE TRADITIONAL & HUMAN SECURITY MATRIX

FOUR CORE PILLARS OF SECURITY THEORY & PRACTICE

1. Traditional External
Military threats from states → Deterrence, defense, balance of power, alliance pacts (NATO, Warsaw).
2. Disarmament & Arms Control
Biological Weapons Convention (1972); Chemical Weapons Convention (1992); NPT (1968); START treaties.
3. Non-Traditional / Human
Referent is individuals; Freedom from Fear & Want; Global pandemics, poverty, forced migration, terrorism.
4. India's 4-Pillar Strategy
Military strength; International norms & treaties; Domestic democratic accommodation; Economic growth.

Traditional vs Non-Traditional Security

Referent Object: Traditional secures the state and sovereignty; Non-traditional secures individual human beings.
Nature of Threats: Traditional deals with military aggression and war; Non-traditional deals with disease, climate, and hunger.
Primary Actor: Traditional relies on national militaries and intelligence; Non-traditional requires international cooperation.
Use of Force: Military force is central to traditional defense; force is largely futile against pandemics or climate change.
Scope: Traditional is narrow and interstate; Non-traditional encompasses global and transnational civil society.

Key Disarmament & Arms Control Treaties

Biological Weapons Convention (BWC, 1972): Banned the development, production, and stockpiling of biological weapons.
Chemical Weapons Convention (CWC, 1992): Completely outlawed chemical weapons; mandated verifiable destruction of stockpiles.
Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT, 1968): Froze nuclear status to states testing prior to 1 January 1967; India refused to sign.
Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty (1972): Limited US and USSR from deploying nationwide ballistic missile shield defenses.
START I & II (1991, 1993): Strategic Arms Reduction Treaties mandating verified reductions in nuclear warhead stockpiles.

CHRONOLOGY OF GLOBAL DISARMAMENT, CONVENTIONS & PARADIGM SHIFTS

1968

NPT Signed: 1 Jan 1967 cutoff; divides world into nuclear haves & have-nots. India boycotts as discriminatory.

1972

BWC & ABM: Biological weapons banned; US and USSR sign Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty in Moscow.

1991–1993

Cold War Drawdown: START-I reduces strategic nuclear warheads; Chemical Weapons Convention (CWC) adopted in 1992.

1994

Human Security: UNDP Human Development Report formally coins 'Human Security' following Rwandan genocide.

11 Sept 2001

9/11 Terror Attacks: Al-Qaeda attacks WTC/Pentagon; terrorism recognized as premier global transnational threat.

2020+

Pandemic Insecurity: COVID-19 pandemic kills millions, shutting down global economy and highlighting health security.

INDIA'S FOURFOLD NATIONAL SECURITY STRATEGY

1. Strengthening Military Defense Deterring nuclear and conventional threats from Pakistan and China. Testing nuclear devices in 1974 (Pokhran-I) and 1998 (Pokhran-II) to build a "credible minimum nuclear deterrent" with a strict No-First-Use doctrine.
2. Strengthening International Norms Championing universal, non-discriminatory disarmament, Asian solidarity, the Non-Aligned Movement (NAM), and active participation in United Nations Peacekeeping Operations to preserve international rule of law.
3. Preserving Internal Unity & Cohesion Accommodating regional, linguistic, and ethnic autonomy demands (Northeast, Kashmir, Punjab) through federalism, democratic elections, and constitutional flexibility to prevent internal balkanization.

PITFALL 1: Refugee vs Internally Displaced Person (IDP)

A Refugee leaves their country and flees across international borders. An IDP flees their home due to violence or disaster but remains within the national borders of their own country.

PITFALL 2: Disarmament vs Arms Control

Disarmament requires states to give up certain kinds of weapons altogether (e.g., BWC, CWC). Arms Control regulates the acquisition, testing, or size of weapon stockpiles (e.g., NPT, SALT, START).

PITFALL 3: India's Opposition to the NPT

India refused to sign the NPT (1968) NOT because it opposed non-proliferation, but because the treaty was fundamentally discriminatory, creating an arbitrary cutoff date of 1 January 1967.
