FISCAL OVERLORDSHIP
The Mughal crown exercised fiscal authority through the Amil-guzar (chief revenue collector), assisted by the Qanungo (keeper of land records) and Fotadar (treasurer). Land revenue was classified as Jama (assessed) and Hasil (collected). Cash payment was encouraged.
Zamindars: Feudal Intermediaries & Milkiyat
HEREDITARY GENTRY
Zamindars held hereditary proprietary land called milkiyat cultivated for private profit. They extracted revenue on behalf of the state for a fixed commission, maintained armed retainers (qilachas, cavalry, musketeers), and bought/sold zamindari rights via commercial deeds.
Village Oligarchy: Muqaddam, Patwari & Panchayat
VILLAGE COMMUNITY
The village community was governed by an assembly of caste elders (Panchayat) headed by the Muqaddam (or Mandal), whose appointment was ratified by the zamindar. The village accountant (Patwari) maintained records financed by a common village fund (khisam).
Peasantry: Khud-kasht vs Pahi-kasht Duality
NTA HIGH-YIELD DISTINCTION
Khud-kasht: Resident peasants cultivating their own ancestral land in their village. Pahi-kasht: Non-resident cultivators tilling land in other villages on a contractual basis, often driven by lower revenue rates, natural disasters, or debt distress.
Kamins & Village Artisans: Jajmani Reciprocity
HEREDITARY RECIPROCITY
Artisans (blacksmiths, carpenters, potters) rendered customary repair and fabrication services to cultivators in exchange for shares of harvest or small land allotments (known as watan or miras in Maharashtra; kamins in North India). Menial caste laborers were excluded from panchayats.
