Colonial Revenue Systems: Permanent Settlement vs Ryotwari
Comparative institutional framework: legal property definitions, fiscal realization, zamindari dynamics, and rural peasant credit
OURMOCKS STUDY VISUALS
CUET UG History · Unit 9
Permanent Settlement (1793)
Bengal, Bihar & Orissa
  • Architect: Introduced by Lord Charles Cornwallis to secure fixed, predictable revenues for the East India Company.
  • Property Conversion: Transformed traditional rajas and taluqdars into legal proprietors of the soil (Zamindars).
  • Invariable State Demand: Revenue demand was fixed in perpetuity; state could never claim future agricultural improvements.
  • The Sunset Law: If revenue dues were not paid by sunset of the specified cutoff date, the zamindari estate was auctioned.
  • Rise of Jotedars: Rich resident peasants (known as haoladars or gantidars) in North Bengal acquired extensive land and manipulated auctions.
Zamindars' Survival Strategies
Zamindars countered auctions through benami (fictitious) transactions—transferring property to women (e.g. Burdwan Raja's mother) or utilizing agents to bid up prices and intentionally default, forcing continuous re-auctions.
Ryotwari System (1820s)
Bombay Deccan & Madras
  • Ideological Roots: Influenced by David Ricardo’s economic rent theory; Company avoided creating parasitic feudal intermediaries.
  • Direct State-Peasant Contract: Revenue settlement made directly with the individual cultivator (ryot) as registered landholder.
  • Periodic Revision: Unlike Bengal, land was surveyed, soil classified, and revenue rates reassessed every 30 years.
  • Exorbitant Demand: Initial assessments in the 1820s demanded nearly 50% of gross produce, triggering peasant flight and debt traps.
  • Moneylender Predation: Cultivators relied on moneylenders (sahukars / vanis) to pay rigid cash installments during crop failure.
Deccan Agrarian Crisis & Riots (1875)
Collapse of the Cotton Boom (1861–65) following the American Civil War, combined with the Limitation Law of 1859, led sahukars to refuse credit. Peasants revolted at Supa, burning debt bonds and account books (bahikhatas).
The Fifth Report (1813)
Submitted to British Parliament by a select committee; highlighted zamindari auction distress to lobby against the Company's trade monopoly.
Jotedars vs Zamindars
While zamindars lived in urban mansions, jotedars lived in villages directly controlling sharecroppers (adhiyars / bargadars).
Deccan Riots Commission (1878)
Official inquiry concluded that peasant distress stemmed from usurious moneylenders rather than excessive colonial revenue assessments.
