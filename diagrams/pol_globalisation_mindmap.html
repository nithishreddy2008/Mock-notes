GLOBALISATION AND ITS CRITICS
CONTEMPORARY WORLD POLITICS · CH. 7 (TOPIC 16)

1. Concept & Technological Drivers

Multidimensional Flow: Interconnected movement of ideas, capital, commodities, and people across borders.
Technological Catalyst: Microchips, telegraph, satellite telecom, and internet revolutionized global communications.
Historical Continuity: Existed through ancient trade routes (Silk Road), but modern scale and speed are unprecedented.
Global Consciousness: Events occurring in one corner of the earth trigger immediate worldwide consequences.

2. Political Consequences

Erosion of State Capacity: Rise of market forces retreats welfare functions; state focuses on core law & order.
Primacy of the State: State sovereignty is not dead; states retain supreme political authority and border policing.
Enhanced Administrative Power: Digital surveillance, biometric data, and intelligence networks make states more powerful.
Corporate Influence: Multinational corporations and credit rating agencies limit domestic economic policymaking.

3. Economic Manifestations

Global Trade Expansion: Tariff reductions, currency convertibility, and FDI facilitated by WTO and IMF.
Distributional Disparities: Creates winners (technocrats, MNCs) and losers (small unorganized artisans, farm laborers).
Asymmetry of Flows: Capital and manufactured goods move freely, but human labor migration faces strict Western visa walls.
Safety Nets: Advocates urge targeted social safety nets to cushion vulnerable populations against market shocks.

4. Cultural Dynamics: Homogenisation

Cultural Homogenisation: Spread of uniform Western consumer culture; "McDonaldization", Hollywood, and blue jeans.
Cultural Imperialism: Threatens distinctive local indigenous traditions, languages, and traditional art forms.
Cultural Heterogenisation: Blending of foreign influences with local practices (e.g. masala dosa burgers, khadi kurtas over jeans).
Dynamic Evolution: Cultures are not static museum pieces; external contact can expand human choices and enrich traditions.

5. Anti-Globalisation Movements

Leftist Critique: Denounces corporate neo-liberalism, labor casualization, and privatization of basic health and water.
Rightist Critique: Fears erosion of cultural purity, traditional family values, and national economic self-reliance.
1999 Seattle Protests: Massive civil society disruption shut down the WTO Ministerial Conference in Seattle.
World Social Forum (WSF): Global coalition formed under the slogan "Another World is Possible" (Porto Alegre 2001, Mumbai 2004).

6. India & Struggles Against Globalisation

Historical Backdrop: British colonial drain of raw materials prompted post-1947 protective "swadeshi" and license raj.
1991 LPG Turnaround: Balance-of-payments crisis forced dismantling of licensing and opening to foreign capital.
Trade Union Resistance: Central unions (CITU, AITUC) staged national strikes against PSU privatization and labor contractization.
Farmer Struggles: Farmers mobilized against MNC patenting of indigenous seeds (Neem, Basmati) and WTO subsidies.

EXAM PITFALLS — WSF, SEATTLE & CONCEPTS

World Social Forum (WSF): 1st meeting in Porto Alegre, Brazil (2001); 4th meeting in Mumbai, India (2004)!
Cultural Heterogenisation: Does NOT mean complete Westernization; it refers to the hybridisation and blending of cultures!
1999 Seattle Protests: Targeted the WTO Ministerial Conference, NOT the UN or World Bank!
