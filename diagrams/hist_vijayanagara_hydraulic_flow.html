Systems flow analyzing how riverine dams, irrigation canals, and 7-tier fortification walls ensured urban survival during long medieval sieges

Stage 1 · Catchment
Natural Basin
Tungabhadra River Basin
The natural granite hill amphitheater formed a rugged natural fortress. Rulers constructed stone embankments across rainwater streams flowing down into the river to form catchment reservoirs.
Stage 2 · Distribution
Canals & Tanks
Hiriya Canal & Kamalapuram
Kamalapuram tank stored water for paddy fields and piped supplies to the Royal Centre; the Hiriya canal diverted river water across dams to irrigate the central agricultural valley.
Stage 3 · Fortification
7 Concentric Walls
Enclosing Agriculture
First tier of walls linked rocky hills to enclose vast swathes of cultivated fields, orchards, and canals. Subsequent tiers encircled the urban quarters and the royal palaces.
Stage 4 · Defense
Anti-Starvation
Siege Immunity
Besieging armies could not starve the capital into submission because massive food supplies and perennial freshwater were protected securely inside the fortified perimeter.
1. Why Enclose Agricultural Fields?
Medieval sieges often lasted months or years. Traditional rulers stored grain in silos, but Vijayanagara incorporated living agricultural fields and perennial canals directly within its defensive walls to guarantee self-sufficiency.
2. Dry Masonry & Interlocking Joints
No mortar or cementing agent was used. Giant granite blocks were wedge-shaped and fitted seamlessly using mortice-and-tenon interlocking joints, with earthen rubble packed between stone faces for artillery absorption.
3. European & Persian Testimonies
Persian envoy Abdur Razzaq marveled that between the 1st and 3rd walls were cultivated fields and gardens; Portuguese visitor Domingo Paes noted that rice and wheat grew inside the city.
Key Strategic Innovation: Enclosing the agricultural base inside the perimeter walls neutralized the primary weapon of medieval siege warfare: famine.
